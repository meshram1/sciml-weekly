The theme this week is **how much a learned PDE model can be trusted, and how cheaply it can be trained**. INO attaches
an error bound to each prediction, CasEm and Transolver-σ target long-rollout accuracy, and the last three papers ask how
to get more from pretraining or from cheaper low-dimensional data.

This was a quiet week for software releases: I did not find a new release of the tools I track, so there is no release
item. For every paper below I opened the arXiv abstract page. The summaries rely on the abstracts only; I have not read the
full papers, so details beyond them are left out.

## Concept of the week: residual-based error bounds

A neural surrogate normally gives you an answer with no indication of how wrong it is. For many PDEs there is a way to
check an answer without knowing the true solution: plug it back into the equation and see how badly the equation is violated.
That leftover is the **residual**.

Take a linear problem written in weak form, $a(u,v) = \ell(v)$ for all test functions $v$. For a candidate $\tilde u$, the
residual is the functional $r(v) = \ell(v) - a(\tilde u, v)$. If the problem is **coercive** (the bilinear form $a$ is bounded
below by a constant $\alpha > 0$ times the squared norm), a standard result gives

$$\|u - \tilde u\| \;\le\; \frac{\|r\|_{*}}{\alpha},$$

where $\|r\|_*$ is the size of the residual. The right-hand side can be computed without the true $u$. This is the textbook
(a posteriori) form of the idea; the exact bound used in item 1 is in the paper, which I have not read.

Physics-informed training minimises a residual, but a small *average* residual on training points does not certify any single
prediction. Item 1 builds the certificate into the method.

## 1. INO: a neural operator with a per-prediction error bound

**Paper:** *Interpolating Neural Operator (INO): A Data-Free and Efficient Approach for Learning PDE Solution Operators*. Jiachen Guo, Ye Lu, Naichen Shi, Thomas J.R. Hughes, Wing Kam Liu, arXiv, 29 Sep 2026.[^ino]

**The idea in plain words.** Most neural operators either need many simulations as training data or a long
physics-informed training run on GPUs, and they cannot say how accurate one particular prediction is. INO needs no simulation
data, trains on one CPU core, and gives a bound for each answer.

**What's technically new.** INO is trained directly on the weak form of the PDE. The input field is represented by
Karhunen-Loève coordinates (a few numbers that summarise the field), which are fed to the network as extra inputs next to the
spatial coordinates. Each input is approximated by a C-HiDeNN sub-network whose trainable parameters are nodal values. Because
the network is multilinear in those parameters, training becomes a sequence of one-dimensional linear solves (greedy
alternating least squares). For coercive problems, every prediction has a computable residual bound that needs no reference
solution. Before predicting, INO also checks whether the leading input coordinates lie in the trained range, and otherwise
suggests falling back to a conventional solver.[^ino]

**Result.** The authors compare with physics-informed FNO and DeepONet. They report INO is the most accurate model on most
problems, by 15× on 2D Helmholtz at $65^2$ and 53× on the diffusion-reaction benchmark. On the one- and two-dimensional problems,
training on one CPU core takes 3-80× less time than the physics-informed baselines on one GPU.[^ino]

> **Why it matters:** An out-of-range check plus a certified error is what an engineer needs to decide when to trust a
> surrogate. The open question the abstract does not answer is how this scales to the nonlinear, 3D, turbulent problems that
> dominate CFD. The bound is stated for coercive problems.

## 2. CasEm: guide a drifting emulator with a small side model

**Paper:** *CasEm: A Cascade Architecture for Long-Horizon Neural Emulation*. Zhaoyi Li, Jingtao Ding, Shihua Li, arXiv, 28 Sep 2026.[^casem]

**The idea in plain words.** Autoregressive emulators can drift over long rollouts (see the concept in [issue 1](issue-01.html)).
CasEm adds a second, separate model that tracks a few **physically specified aggregates** (for example, the total water in a
region), evolving on its own. Its forecasts are used to correct the full-state prediction. Information flows one way only: from
the aggregate model to the backbone, never back.

**What's technically new.** The authors derive a finite-horizon error bound that names three requirements for the aggregates: they
must cover a substantial part of the backbone error, be accurately predictable, and support useful corrections. They use
empirical diagnostics to pick which subsystem to model.[^casem]

**Result.** Across four ODE/PDE benchmarks, CasEm reduces long-horizon rollout error for several backbones and suppresses error
divergence in both diffusion tasks with Fourier neural operator backbones. In global climate emulation, with a regional
total-water subsystem, the 10-year full-state time-mean error falls by 66.6% for a frozen ACE backbone and by 46.3% for
Spherical DYffusion, adding less than 3% to inference time.[^casem]

> **Why it matters:** It is a drop-in add-on to an existing emulator rather than a new architecture, and it ties the choice of
> corrective variable to a bound. The aggregates must still be chosen with domain knowledge.

## 3. Transolver-σ: joint spectral and physical subspaces

**Paper:** *Transolver-σ: Joint Spectral-Physical Subspace Modeling for Neural PDE Solving*. Haonan Shangguan, Hang Zhou, Haixu Wu, Yuezhou Ma, Jianmin Wang, Mingsheng Long, arXiv, 29 Sep 2026.[^sigma]

**The idea in plain words.** The authors observe that a solver working only in physical space can have lower one-step error,
while a spectral-only solver can be more accurate at later rollout steps. So each block uses both.

**What's technically new.** Inside each block, physical-state interactions and spectral transformations live in separate latent
subspaces whose responses are recombined. In the physical subspace they add Slice-Residual Physics-Attention (SRPA), which
keeps an explicit identity path in slice space while still allowing learnable interaction across slices. In parallel, an
axis-factorised Fourier operator captures global spectral structure. It builds on the earlier Transolver (see the
[timeline](../releases.html)).[^sigma]

**Result.** Across five PDE benchmarks (steady-state and time-dependent), the authors report a benchmark-averaged relative error
reduction of 33.4% over the strongest baseline for each metric, and improved autoregressive rollout over single-operator
counterparts. They also report gains on coupled multiphysics systems and on real fluid and combustion measurements from RealPDEBench.[^sigma]

> **Why it matters:** Using both views is a simple design idea that targets rollout stability. As always, the 33.4% is an
> average over the authors' chosen benchmarks.

## 4. PDE-JEPA: pretrain by predicting in latent space

**Paper:** *PDE-JEPA: Predictive Representation Learning of Latent Dynamics Modeling for Parametric PDEs*. Zhentao Tan, Jianrong Zhang, Ruijie Quan, Yi Yang, arXiv, 28 Sep 2026.[^jepa]

**The idea in plain words.** Most PDE representation learning trains an encoder to reconstruct the observed field. Here the
encoder is trained to predict masked parts of the trajectory in latent space (the JEPA idea: joint-embedding predictive
architecture). The authors find that such representations keep rich physical information, but that this alone does not give
accurate field evolution.

**What's technically new.** Three stages: (1) a masked-latent prediction encoder; (2) a geometry projector that aligns the
geometry of latent trajectories with that of the physical fields; (3) a physics-structured latent predictor that splits the
dynamics into a parameter-independent evolution part and a parameter-dependent response part.[^jepa]

**Result.** On nine widely used PDE benchmarks, the authors report an average improvement of 33.4% in-distribution over existing
state-of-the-art methods, and 51.4% on average when extrapolating to unseen governing parameters. There is a project page.[^jepa]

> **Why it matters:** The extrapolation-to-new-parameters number is the one to watch, since that is where surrogates usually fail.

## 5. SPEAR: fewer, less redundant experts for PDE pretraining

**Paper:** *SPEAR: A Spectral-Disentangled MoE Neural Operator with Knowledge-Guided Expert Aggregation for Large-Scale PDE Pretraining*. Dengdi Sun, Xiaoya Zhou, Xiao Wang, Wanli Lyu, Jin Tang, Bin Luo, arXiv, 2 Oct 2026.[^spear]

**The idea in plain words.** A PDE foundation model must handle very different dynamics. One shared representation can cause
"knowledge interference", while a mixture-of-experts (MoE, where a router sends each input to a few specialist sub-networks)
tends to end up with redundant experts.

**What's technically new.** SPEAR splits latent features into low- and high-frequency parts, so transferable dynamics are shared
and PDE-specific patterns are learned separately. A knowledge-guided aggregation step measures how similar experts are, using
dataset-specific learned knowledge and routing preferences, and merges similar ones.[^spear]

**Result.** On twelve PDE datasets and several downstream benchmarks, the authors report better pretraining, fine-tuning and
transfer results, while the aggregation cuts the number of experts by 50% with accuracy maintained or improved. The abstract
gives no other headline numbers.[^spear]

> **Why it matters:** Foundation models are costly to serve, and pruning half the experts without losing accuracy is a direct
> efficiency win. It echoes the distillation result (TREX) in [issue 1](issue-01.html).

## 6. Train in 2D, run in 3D: any-dimensional warm-starting

**Paper:** *Warm-starting PDE solvers with any-dimensional machine learning*. Wilson G. Gregory, George A. Kevrekidis, Ben Blum-Smith, Soledad Villar, arXiv, 30 Sep 2026.[^anyd]

**The idea in plain words.** Some models, such as graph neural networks, can be applied to inputs of any size or dimension. The
authors give mathematical conditions, based on symmetries of the PDE and of the initial data, under which a solver trained in
low dimension can be applied in higher dimension without retraining. When the equation has the symmetry but the data does not
(common in physics), the theory gives a principled way to warm-start a higher-dimensional solver.[^anyd]

**Result.** Tested on the heat equation, Burgers' equation and compressible Navier-Stokes, with gains in both zero-shot and
ordinary training. The headline example: a surrogate trained on 2D Navier-Stokes data beats a baseline trained on 3D data when
tested on 3D, using only 12% of the flops and 20% of the total data size.[^anyd]

> **Why it matters:** 3D training data is the expensive part of CFD surrogates. Cheap 2D data that transfers is attractive,
> though the abstract covers only the listed equations.
