The thread this week is **scale**. A model trained to minimise mean squared error (MSE) tends to produce smooth
predictions that miss the smallest structures. Four of the six items are different ways of getting those structures
back: generate them with a diffusion or flow-matching model, learn the dynamics natively at high resolution, or add a
spectral split to the architecture. The other two are about long rollouts, following up the drift discussion in
[issue 1](issue-01.html).

## Concept of the week: why MSE-trained models look blurry

Suppose a model $f_\theta$ predicts the next state $u_{t+1}$ from the current state $u_t$ and is trained with the squared
error

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\,\big\| f_\theta(u_t) - u_{t+1} \big\|^2 .$$

The function that minimises this loss is the **conditional mean**, $f^\star(u_t) = \mathbb{E}[\,u_{t+1} \mid u_t\,]$. If the
small scales of the next state cannot be fully determined from the current (possibly coarse) state, as happens in
turbulence, storms and ocean eddies, different plausible futures disagree on those details. Averaging over them cancels
the details out. The prediction is then smoother than any single real state, and its energy is too low at high
wavenumbers (small scales).

Three remedies appear in this issue:

1. **Add a generative stage** that samples the missing detail given the smooth prediction (ReAl, HiRO-ACEv2).
2. **Train at the target resolution** instead of coarse-then-downscale (the ocean study).
3. **Change the architecture** so that different scales are handled by different parts of the model (the Hodge-Laplacian experts).

The price of generation is that it adds randomness, and in an autoregressive loop random errors can accumulate. Several
items below are about managing that trade-off.

## 1. Aurora fine-tuned to emulate atmospheric chemistry and climate

**Paper:** *Atmospheric Chemistry-Climate Modeling with an AI Foundation Model*. James Weber and Makoto Kelp, arXiv, 15 Oct 2026.[^aurora]

**The idea in plain words.** Chemistry and aerosol processes are typically the most expensive part of an atmospheric
model, so they are often simplified or prescribed. The authors take Aurora, a pretrained Earth-system foundation model,
and fine-tune it on simulations from UKCA, a chemistry-climate model. The result emulates meteorology together with
composition, aerosols and radiation.

**What's technically new.** The authors describe it as the first foundation model to capture interactions between
emissions, atmospheric composition and radiation. Two training choices stand out. Training on **paired emissions
scenarios** is meant to teach the model how the system *responds* to a change in forcing, not only how it behaves under
one fixed scenario. The model is also trained with **MLS satellite ozone retrievals**, which corrects biases in the
UKCA fields it learned from.

**Result.** All numbers below are from the paper's abstract.[^aurora]

| Property | Reported result |
|---|---|
| Fields reproduced | 23 of 27 predicted fields have $R^2 > 0.85$ in both troposphere and stratosphere |
| Ozone, CO, temperature | annual mean biases below 4% |
| Downwelling shortwave and longwave flux | within 6% |
| Autoregressive stability | stable over 50 years |
| Speed | about 100× faster than UKCA |
| Response to forcing | captures tropospheric ozone's response to NO and CO emission changes, and surface radiation's response to aerosol |
| Adding MLS ozone data | reduces zonal mean ozone error against collocated observations by 23% between 250 and 20 hPa |

> **Why it matters:** Foundation models for weather are being reused for new tasks, and this is a hard one because
> chemistry adds many coupled fields. The authors are explicit that the approach "does not suggest process-based modeling
> can be replaced." What it shows is that a pretrained model can be adapted to respond to forcings at a small fraction of
> the cost.

## 2. HiRO-ACEv2: emulating a 3 km global storm-resolving model

**Paper:** *Extreme events and statistics in an AI emulator of a global storm-resolving model*. Anna Kwa, W. Andre Perkins,
Troy Arcomano, Noah D. Brenowitz, Christopher S. Bretherton, Spencer K. Clark, Lucas M. Harris, Jeremy McGibbon,
Michael S. Pritchard, Oliver Watt-Meyer, arXiv, 13 Oct 2026.[^hiro]

**The idea in plain words.** HiRO-ACEv2 (High Resolution Output Ai2 Climate Emulator) is a two-stage framework that emulates
X-SHiELD, a 3 km global storm-resolving model (GSRM). Stage one is an autoregressive emulator of X-SHiELD coarsened to
100 km. Stage two is a separately trained **diffusion model** that downscales its multivariate outputs to 3 km over a
region. This is the "generate the missing detail" remedy from the concept section.

**What's technically new.** The version 2 model produces coherent precipitation, 10 m winds and sea level pressure,
where version 1 only produced precipitation. The main emphasis is on the **tails** of the distributions, which is where
extreme events live. Tail matching is improved with multiple denoisers at inference time, plus changes to data sampling,
noise sampling and loss weighting during training.[^hiro]

**Result.** Inference costs over 300 times less than the numerical GSRM. The emulator "closely matches" the
high-resolution reference distributions of precipitation and near-surface winds out to their 99.9999th percentile values,
and "broadly tracks" the distribution of tropical cyclone intensities.[^hiro] The abstract gives no more precise numbers
for the cyclone result.

> **Why it matters:** Studying rare extremes needs very large ensembles, and a 3 km global simulation is too expensive to run
> that many times. An emulator that gets the *statistics* of extremes right, not just typical weather, is what makes such
> ensembles useful. Whether it holds beyond the training model's own climate is a question the abstract does not address.

## 3. Ocean emulation: learn at the native resolution

**Paper:** *Learning native eddy-resolving ocean dynamics outperforms coarse emulation and downscaling*. Zilu Liu, Tao Zhang,
Hailong Liu, Kai Xu, Qinghui Lin, Maoxue Yu, Jiangfeng Yu, Jingwei Xie, Zhao Jing, Minghua Zhang, Lixin Wu, arXiv,
10 Oct 2026.[^ocean]

This paper tests the concept directly. The question is whether you can evolve the ocean at coarse resolution and then
downscale, or whether the dynamics must be learned from native high-resolution data. The authors built two
emulators as baselines: a full-depth 1° emulator and a native 0.1° sea surface temperature (SST) emulator. Both stayed
stable in long free-running simulations without significant drift.

| Approach | What the abstract reports |
|---|---|
| Learn dynamics directly at 0.1° | preserves mesoscale energy, spectral characteristics and frontal gradients |
| Evolve at 1°, then downscale | recovers large-scale patterns but fails to reproduce key mesoscale signatures |

The authors also report that the learned mesoscale statistics are consistent across diverse hardware platforms.[^ocean]

> **Why it matters:** Note the contrast with HiRO-ACEv2 above. That system *does* use coarse emulation plus downscaling,
> for the atmosphere, and works for the statistics it targets. This paper finds the same recipe insufficient for ocean
> mesoscale variability. The two are not directly comparable (different media, different targets), but together they say that
> the answer depends on what must be right: tail statistics of a field, or the coherent dynamics of eddies and fronts. The
> authors conclude that high-resolution physical ocean models remain needed as training data.

## 4. ReAl: refine a pretrained PDE surrogate, then align it for rollouts

**Paper:** *ReAl: Refine neural operators via aligning dynamical flow*. Jing Xu, Zenglin Xu, Li Yi, Ruoxi Jiang, Yuan Qi, arXiv, 14 Oct 2026.[^real]

**The idea in plain words.** MSE-trained PDE surrogates capture the dominant dynamics but lose high-frequency detail.
Generative models such as flow matching can capture the detail, but their random errors can accumulate over an
autoregressive rollout. ReAl (Refine and Align) is a **model-agnostic plug-in** that adds a generative stage on top of
an already-trained surrogate.

**What's technically new.** There are two stages:

1. **Refine.** A conditional flow-matching model learns the *residual distribution* around the deterministic prediction. It
   adds back the missing fine scales while the base model keeps the dominant dynamics.
2. **Align.** To limit the generative discrepancies that are amplified in rollouts, especially for chaotic systems, the
   method generates multiple trajectories from the same input and reinforces the refiner with **trajectory-level rewards**.

**Result.** According to the abstract, experiments across diverse surrogate architectures and PDE tasks show that ReAl
"consistently improves high-frequency fidelity, forecasting accuracy, and long-horizon stability."[^real] The abstract gives
no numbers, so I do not quote any; the paper's tables would be the place to look.

> **Why it matters:** Because the refiner sits on top of a pretrained model, you could in principle keep a surrogate you
> already trust and fix its spectrum, instead of retraining. The reward-based alignment step is the interesting part: it
> treats rollout quality as something to optimise directly rather than hoping one-step training transfers.

## 5. ALR: states reached by different routes should agree

**Paper:** *Same Target, Different Origins: Aligning Latent Rollout for Consistent PDE Emulation*. Xinying Li, Jing Xu,
Wenjun Wang, Limei Han, Fenglei Cao, Ruoxi Jiang, Yuan Qi, arXiv, 15 Oct 2026.[^alr]

**The idea in plain words.** For a well-posed deterministic PDE, the state at a given target time is the same no matter
which earlier time you start from. A learned solver does not guarantee this: predicting time $T$ from $t=0$ in ten steps
and from $t=5$ in five steps generally gives different answers, because errors depend on the rollout depth. ALR (Align
Latent Rollout) is a **regulariser** that pushes these answers to agree, measured in the solver's internal representations.

**What's technically new.** A second-order Taylor analysis of how latent discrepancies propagate motivates two terms:

- **Zeroth-order latent state consistency:** representations at a shared target time should match.
- **First-order latent dynamics consistency:** the local response to a shared perturbation should match.

ALR does not change the backbone, so it can be attached to different neural PDE solvers.[^alr]

**Result.** Across multiple PDE benchmarks and neural-operator backbones, ALR "improves prediction accuracy while reducing
shared-target discrepancies across rollout origins."[^alr] The abstract reports no numbers.

> **Why it matters:** ReAl and ALR come from overlapping author lists (Jing Xu, Ruoxi Jiang and Yuan Qi appear on both) and
> attack rollout error from different sides: ReAl through the output distribution, ALR through consistency of the internal
> state. Both are cheap to attach to an existing model, which is a good property for methods like this.

## 6. Hodge-Laplacian mixture of experts for steady flows on meshes

**Paper:** *Learnable Hodge-Laplacian Driven Mixture of Experts for Steady-PDE Surrogates on Coarse Unstructured Meshes*.
Stavros Nousias, Akis Nousias, Konstantinos Gkrispanis, arXiv, 14 Oct 2026 (this is v2 on the abstract page).[^hodge]

This is the architecture remedy. Instead of one mixing mechanism for the whole solution, the model splits the solution
among vanilla transformer **experts**, each focused on a different spectral scale. The split is built from a discrete
Hodge Laplacian, which acts as a layer measuring high-frequency content, and a gating network that takes the mesh as
input produces per-vertex weights to recombine the experts' outputs.

On a reduced, remeshed version of AirFRANS (a steady RANS airfoil benchmark; each mesh has 9.6K faces and 4.9K vertices),
the authors report a 31% reduction in relative $L_2$ error and a 47% reduction in interior mean squared error compared
with Transolver on the same dataset and split.[^hodge]

> **Caveat:** The evaluation is on a reduced version of the benchmark and, from the abstract, against one baseline. It is a
> promising idea, but I would wait for broader comparisons before drawing conclusions.
