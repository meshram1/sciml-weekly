The theme this week is **reuse**. Training a neural surrogate is expensive, and the usual weakness is that it must be redone when the
pipe network, the part geometry, the domain size or the controller changes. All six items below try to learn something once and
apply it more widely. One caveat on the sources: all of them are preprints posted on the same day, and I have read the abstract
pages only, so the results below are the authors' own claims.

## Concept of the week: locality, and why "learn small, run big" is possible

Most PDEs are *local*. In a discretised model, the residual at one grid point or element depends only on its immediate neighbours:

$$r_i(u) \;=\; \frac{u_i^{n+1}-u_i^{n}}{\Delta t} \;-\; f\!\left(u_{i-1},\,u_i,\,u_{i+1}\right) \;=\; 0 .$$

Because the physics at one location only needs nearby information, a model of *a piece of the domain* can in principle be reused
for a bigger domain, as long as the pieces agree where they touch. That agreement is the hard part. Classical domain
decomposition methods spend much of their effort on the interfaces between pieces, because two neighbouring pieces can each store
a slightly different value of the same shared quantity.

Three ideas recur this week:

- **Reuse the learned piece, enforce the physics at the seams.** Item 2 does this for model reduction. Item 1 does it for pipe networks.
- **Share the learned piece, not the data.** Item 3 does this for companies that cannot pool their geometries.
- **Make the piece cheap to run in parallel.** Item 4 does this for PINNs.

## 1. A neural operator for sewer hydraulics that transfers to unseen networks

**Paper:** *Generalized hydraulic neural operators turbocharging sewer digital twins*. Lin, Li, Shi, Hu, Zhang, Tian & Yuan, arXiv, Nov 13, 2026.[^sewer]

**The idea in plain words.** A *digital twin* of a sewer network is a model that runs alongside the real system and is kept in sync
with sensor data. Running it normally means solving the Saint-Venant equations (the standard 1D equations for free-surface flow in
channels and pipes), which is too slow for real-time use on a large network. Earlier neural surrogates were fast but tied to one
pipe or network, so every new pipe meant a new training run.

**What's technically new.** The authors build a "fast, generalized and differentiable multi-branch neural operator" trained on
solutions of the Saint-Venant equations. Multiple branches (the abstract does not say which inputs each branch takes) let one
operator handle different pipes and flow conditions. Because it is differentiable, it can be used inside data assimilation and
diagnostics, not only forward prediction.[^sewer]

**Result.** The abstract reports thousand- to million-fold speedups for online prediction, data assimilation and diagnostics, and
generalisation to unseen pipes, networks and flow conditions without retraining. It gives no accuracy numbers in the abstract, so
check the paper's tables before relying on the "high fidelity" claim.[^sewer]

> **Why it matters:** Hydraulic digital twins only help if they run faster than the storm. Generalising across pipes is the
> difference between a research demo and something a utility can deploy on its own network without a training team.

## 2. Bottom-up model reduction without interface bases

**Paper:** *Compatible by Construction: Bottom-Up Nonlinear Model Reduction without Interface Bases*. Kim & Choi, arXiv, Nov 13, 2026.[^rom]

**The idea in plain words.** A *reduced-order model* (ROM) replaces the full discretised PDE with a few unknowns that describe
the solution on a low-dimensional **manifold**: a decoder network maps the few coordinates to the full state. The cost is in
training: learning such a decoder on a *large* domain needs large-domain data. The authors instead learn the decoder on a
**small training domain** and reuse it on bigger ones.

**What's technically new.** Reuse across subdomains normally creates a seam problem (see the concept above). Here, a shared
nonlinear decoder represents *all* the degrees of freedom owned by a subdomain, with no separate interior and interface models.
Each global degree of freedom has a **unique owner**, and neighbouring subdomains' residuals read the decoded value from the owner.
So the discrete state is single-valued by construction, with no duplicated interface copies and no extra compatibility
constraints. With the decoder frozen, the per-subdomain reduced coordinates are found jointly by minimising the original
time-discrete PDE residual, using a Gauss–Newton system assembled from local contributions.[^rom]

**Result.** On parameterised viscous Burgers and incompressible Navier–Stokes problems, the method reuses the decoder on larger
domains without retraining. Against local proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) models using the same coupling construction, the
authors report "favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-offs in the tested regimes", with no numbers in the abstract.[^rom]

> **Why it matters:** Because the physics is enforced through the original residual, accuracy at the seams does not depend on a
> learned interface model. That is a more trustworthy way to scale up than training a bigger network.

## 3. Fed-PIGANO: train together without sharing geometries

**Paper:** *Federated Physics-Informed Thermal Field Prediction Across Unseen Geometries in Distributed Additive Manufacturing*. Saimon & Kong, arXiv, Nov 13, 2026.[^fed]

**The idea in plain words.** In metal additive manufacturing (3D printing), each new part shape needs a new thermal simulation. A
**geometry-aware neural operator** can predict the temperature field on a new part directly, but it needs diverse training
shapes, and each factory holds only its own proprietary designs. *Federated learning* trains one shared model by exchanging model
weights between sites and never the underlying data.

**What's technically new.** Fed-PIGANO trains physics-informed geometry-aware neural operators (PI-GANO) at each site, then
aggregates their geometry encoders and prediction networks. Part geometries stay local.[^fed]

**Result.** The benchmark is a 2D steady-state thermal problem with geometries from five polygonal families spread across three
sites, in four scenarios from identical to strongly different family mixes. Over seven replications, the mean relative L2 error is
1.83% to 2.04% across all 12 scenario-site cases, against up to 20.46% for local-only training. PDE residuals are also
consistently lower, and accuracy is comparable to a centralised model trained on all geometries pooled.[^fed]

> **Why it matters:** The benchmark is small and 2D, so treat it as a proof of concept. The comparison that matters is
> federated vs local-only: sites with few designs gain a lot from sites with different ones.

## 4. Fuse-PINN: can PINNs scale across GPUs?

**Paper:** *Fuse-PINN: Combining Finite Element Methods and Physics-Informed Neural Networks to enable massively parallel simulations of non-linear PDEs*. Pamela, Pailliez, Franck, Michel-Dansac, Navoret & Gopakumar, arXiv, Nov 13, 2026.[^fuse]

A physics-informed neural network (PINN) trains a network to make the PDE residual small at sampled points. The authors argue
that PINNs are usually shown on toy problems and that "PINNs don't scale" has become a common belief. Their answer is a
formulation called FEM-NN (Finite-Element-Method for Neural Networks), implemented in a modular framework, Fuse-PINN, whose
structure supports parallelising training on GPU-based HPC systems. They add an **Energy Natural Gradient Descent** optimiser for
high-precision convergence. The demonstrations are physics models relevant to magnetic confinement fusion, including non-linear
convective models with fine-scale dynamics, and the abstract reports "near-perfect scaling" on multi-GPU runs as the problem size grows.[^fuse]

> **Caveat:** The arXiv comments field says the paper was "Rejected at ICLR", so it has not passed peer review. The abstract also
> gives no error numbers or GPU counts, so "scales" here means parallel efficiency, not necessarily accuracy on hard turbulence.

## 5. φ-PICT: a differentiable MHD solver for flow control

**Paper:** *Sample-Efficient Control of Magnetoconvective Flows via Differentiable Simulation*. Becktepe, Krasnov, Thuerey, Schumacher & Peitz, arXiv, Nov 13, 2026.[^phipict]

**The idea in plain words.** Active flow control (AFC) means choosing actuations, such as local heating or forcing, that improve
a flow property like heat transfer. Reinforcement learning treats the solver as a black box and needs many simulations. If the
solver is **differentiable**, gradients flow through it and the controller needs far fewer samples. Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD)
flows, which matter for fusion blanket cooling, had not had such a tool.

**What's new.** φ-PICT is a differentiable, GPU-accelerated MHD solver aimed at AFC. It is validated against MHD benchmarks and
integrated into **FluidGym**, an environment for flow-control learning. It is publicly available at
<https://github.com/upb-sciml/phiPICT> (I have not opened the repository; the link is from the paper's abstract).[^phipict]

**Result.** Per the abstract, gradient-based algorithms improve heat transfer by more than 25%, and optimisers reach $10^3\times$
higher accuracy in inverse design than black-box methods.[^phipict]

> **Why it matters:** The pattern, a differentiable solver plus gradient-based optimisation, is the same idea that makes
> neural-operator surrogates attractive for design. Here it is applied to the solver itself.

## 6. One Koopman model for many controllers

**Paper:** *Policy-Conditioned Koopman Representations for Parameterized Closed-Loop Systems*. Krishnan & Deka, arXiv, Nov 13, 2026.[^pck]

**The idea in plain words.** A **Koopman model** lifts a nonlinear system into coordinates (observables) where its dynamics are
approximately linear, which makes prediction and control easier. When a feedback controller is in the loop, changing the controller
parameters changes the closed-loop dynamics, and possibly the best observables too. The usual fix is to refit for each controller.

**What's technically new.** The paper conditions the Koopman representation on the policy, so that both the latent dynamics and
the observable coordinates can change with the controller parameters. A single learned model then predicts a family of
closed-loop trajectories without retraining.[^pck]

**Result.** On four nonlinear prediction benchmarks the model stays below a 15% error threshold on every system, while each
baseline exceeds it on at least one. Used for one-shot feedback-parameter selection, the chosen controllers stabilise every
system on nonlinear control benchmarks, including MuJoCo, with 3 to 97 times less online computation than nonlinear model
predictive control.[^pck]

> **Why it matters:** This is a systems-and-control paper rather than a fluids one, but it is the same reuse idea. For flow
> control, a surrogate that covers a whole family of controllers is what makes design-time search affordable.
