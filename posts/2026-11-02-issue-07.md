The theme this week is **where the physics goes in a learned model, and what happens to it afterwards**. HotPOT hard-wires a
numerical update into each expert, the meshless models build in grid independence, and two studies ask what training does to
a model: fine-tuning can erase what a foundation model knew, and a stabilising trick only pays off under certain conditions.

## Concept of the week: the error amplification ratio

[Issue 1](issue-01.html) explained why autoregressive rollouts drift: each step feeds the model its own slightly wrong output.
But not all error growth is the model's fault. In a chaotic system such as turbulence, even the *true* solver magnifies
tiny differences in the state. The rate is set by the largest **Lyapunov exponent** $\lambda$, and a **Lyapunov time** $1/\lambda$ is
roughly the time over which errors grow by a factor of $e$.

So a rollout error has two sources: the model's one-step mistakes, and how strongly the dynamics stretch those mistakes.
The Jacobian paper in section 3 makes this precise. It propagates the same one-step mistakes through the *solver's* Jacobian $J_{\text{true}}$ to get a
counterfactual error $e_{\text{phys}}$ (what a perfect-dynamics model with those same mistakes would have), then compares it to the emulator's real error:

$$\text{amplification ratio} \;=\; \frac{\|e_{\text{model}}\|}{\|e_{\text{phys}}\|}.$$

A ratio near 1 means the emulator's dynamics behave like the solver's, so the error growth is the physics and cannot be trained away.
A large ratio means the model's own dynamics add extra amplification, which is the part a stabilising regulariser can remove.[^jac]

## 1. HotPOT: experts that end in a numerical update

**Paper:** *HotPOT: Operator Transformer with Heterogeneous Physics Experts for Large-Scale PDE Pre-training*. J. Li, G. Zhou, Y. Luo, R. Chen, P. Jiang, D. Meng, F. D. Salim, P. Koniusz, arXiv, 28 Oct 2026.[^hotpot]

**The idea in plain words.** PDE pretraining trains one model on many equations at once. The abstract notes that adding very different
PDEs "does not always improve predictive performance": the equations interfere with each other. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models reduce this
by routing each input to a specialist sub-network, but their experts all have the same architecture, which does not reflect that
different equations evolve differently.

**What's technically new.** HotPOT keeps a **shared encoder** for what the PDEs have in common. Each PDE family then gets a
"neural-numerical expert": a learnable predictor paired with a **fixed numerical update that has no trainable parameters**. The predictor outputs
the quantities that update needs (think of the terms of a time-stepping scheme), and the update computes the next state. Equations share the encoder but keep their own predictor and update rule.

**Result.** Across five PDE families and six settings, HotPOT beats MoE-POT in all 24 matched-scale comparisons, before and after
fine-tuning, with 50.7% lower rollout error on average. The 17M-parameter model also beats substantially larger MoE-POT models, and
the gains extend to zero-shot prediction under unseen physical conditions. Code is released.[^hotpot]

> **Why it matters:** The fixed numerical update means part of each step is exact arithmetic, not learned. That is one way to
> put physics into a foundation model. The abstract does not say how the gain splits between the numerical updates and the expert design, so treat that as an open question until the ablations are read closely.

## 2. Frozen in Time: what fine-tuning does to Walrus

**Paper:** *Frozen in Time: How a Physics Foundation Model Forgets Under Sim-to-Real Adaptation*. H. Mian, M. Cranmer, A. Favero, P. Mukhopadhyay, arXiv, 29 Oct 2026 (accepted at the Sim2Science workshop, NeurIPS 2026).[^frozen]

**The idea in plain words.** **Catastrophic forgetting** is when a model, adapted to a new task, loses what it knew before.
The authors study it in Walrus-1.3B, the continuum-dynamics foundation model on [the timeline](../releases.html), through a two-stage
sim-to-real adaptation: first fine-tune on simulated flow around a cylinder, then adapt to experimental data of the same system.

**Result.**

| Stage | What the authors report |
|---|---|
| Fine-tune on simulation | One-step errors on the original pretraining datasets rise by up to 35x after only 20 epochs |
| Then adapt to real data | Performance on the simulated task is eroded further |
| Replay 1% simulated data during real-data fine-tuning | Recovers most of the lost simulation performance while still adapting to the experiment |

<small>All figures from the abstract.[^frozen] The authors also characterise drivers such as dataset dependence and task-representation similarity; see the paper.</small>

> **Why it matters:** Foundation models are sold on adapting cheaply to your data. If adaptation quietly breaks the general model,
> you need a retention check, and replay of a small amount of old data is a cheap fix. The study covers one model and one flow, so how far it generalises is not shown.

## 3. When does Jacobian fine-tuning stabilise a chaotic emulator?

**Paper:** *When Does Jacobian Fine-Tuning Stabilize Neural PDE Emulators?* S. Bangera, M. Gupta, arXiv, 30 Oct 2026.[^jac]

**The idea in plain words.** A common stabiliser is to fine-tune the emulator so its Jacobian (how sensitive its output is to its input)
matches the solver's. The paper asks when this actually helps, using the amplification ratio from the concept section.

**Result.** The study covers 21 configurations across four PDEs, five architectures and two model sizes (8-10k and 33k parameters), with errors measured at one Lyapunov time.

| Seeds | Outcome of Jacobian fine-tuning |
|---|---|
| Ratio at most 1.25 (34 seeds) | Reduced error by more than 10% for only 1 of 34 |
| Ratio at least 4 (20 seeds) | Reduced error by more than 10% for all 20; geometric-mean error fell by 8x (small models) and 55x (33k models) |

The authors find the gain "approximately equals the one-step accuracy cost of fine-tuning divided by the amplification ratio".
Matching the Jacobian along the model's own error direction (EDJM) beat random directions at the same cost on 28 of 35 seeds, but plain input-noise injection performed comparably to EDJM.[^jac]

> **Why it matters:** This is a diagnostic as much as a method. Measure the ratio first. If it is near 1, regularising the
> Jacobian is unlikely to help. The models here are tiny, so whether the pattern holds for large emulators is not tested.

## 4. Meshless latent dynamics models

**Paper:** *Meshless Latent Dynamics Models for Model Order Reduction of time-dependent Partial Differential Equations*. L. Tomada, F. Pichi, G. Rozza, arXiv, 29 Oct 2026.[^mldm]

**The idea in plain words.** Model order reduction (MOR) compresses a simulation into a few latent variables that evolve in time. Autoencoder-based
versions are tied to the mesh used in training. The authors instead model the field as a **neural field** (a network that takes a
position and returns a value) *conditioned on a latent state*, so it works on any set of points.

**What's technically new.** A taxonomy of conditioning mechanisms (weak, strong and pure), a universal approximation theorem for this model class, and Lipschitz
bounds on stability. The analysis shows an expressivity-stability trade-off: more expressive conditioning can hurt when extrapolating in time. It also shows that meshless
models have grid-independent Lipschitz bounds, unlike mesh-based ones.

**Result.** On Burgers' equation, Navier-Stokes flow past a cylinder and linear advection, meshless models reach accuracy "comparable to or better than" mesh-based
counterparts using only 2% of the trainable parameters, with nearly constant error across spatial discretisations.[^mldm]

> **Why it matters:** Grid independence is the practical selling point: train on one mesh, query on another. The test cases are classic small problems, so 3D industrial flows remain untested here.

## 5. In-context multi-fidelity learning

**Paper:** *In-Context Learning for Multi-Fidelity Multiscale Model Closure and Cross-Operator Out-Of-Distribution Generalization*. X. Li, M. Zahr, P. Stinis, Z. Zhang, arXiv, 28 Oct 2026.[^icl]

**The idea in plain words.** Multi-fidelity learning combines cheap coarse solutions with a few expensive fine ones. Usually each new PDE means retraining.
Here a single model is trained on paired coarse/fine examples ("demonstrations") from many operators plus a new coarse query, much like in-context learning in language models.

**Result.** For a new operator, fewer than five coarse-fine demonstration pairs and a cheap coarse query are enough to predict the fine solution, without fine-tuning.
The smallest model has 255,364 trainable parameters, about 456 per training operator across 560 operators.[^icl] The abstract gives no accuracy numbers, so see the paper's tables.

> **Why it matters:** It is the in-context idea from foundation models, applied to closure of coarse-scale solvers. That needs a coarse solver already, which is a cost but also a familiar workflow.

## 6. PhysicsNeMo-Mesh: preprocessing at the point of use

**Paper:** *PhysicsNeMo-Mesh: GPU-Native Meshes and Simulation Cases for Physics AI*. P. D. Sharpe, C. Adams, N. Sobhani, S. Choudhry, arXiv, 29 Oct 2026.[^mesh]

**The idea in plain words.** Training on CFD meshes is often limited by I/O and CPU preprocessing, not GPU compute. PhysicsNeMo-Mesh, the mesh module of NVIDIA's open-source PhysicsNeMo library, offers
PyVista-like mesh processing "on the GPU, in batches, and inside PyTorch's automatic differentiation". Fields on points, cells and whole meshes carry names and shapes, and a simulation case stores the full PDE problem including boundary conditions.

**Result (authors' own benchmarks on one GB200 GPU).**

| Measure | Reported |
|---|---|
| Mesh operations vs VTK with 72 threads | 6.2-1,068x faster |
| Loading a whole case vs reading VTK files | 8.0-88x faster (8.0x when both read the same fields) |
| Preprocessing overlapped with training | 2.1% of throughput for a 127 ms Transolver step |
| Heat conduction, shapes and wall conditions both varying | Per-boundary conditions cut error from 87% to 6-9% |

<small>Reported by the paper's authors for their own library (the module belongs to NVIDIA's PhysicsNeMo, so treat these as company-reported); not independently reproduced.[^mesh]</small>

The paper adds that one stored DrivAerML dataset feeds Transolver, GeoTransolver and GLOBE in PhysicsNeMo's public recipe. The mesh module also appears in the [PhysicsNeMo 26.08 release notes](https://docs.nvidia.com/physicsnemo/latest/release-notes/index.html).[^pnemo]

> **Why it matters:** For anyone training mesh-based surrogates, data loading is often the real bottleneck. The speed-ups are large but come from the authors' setup on a very new GPU, so expect smaller gains on other hardware.
