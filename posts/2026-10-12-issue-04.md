The theme this week is **where to put the physics**. Item 1 puts it in the architecture, by deriving the network from a numerical
scheme. Item 2 puts it in the solver: the network only chooses a good subspace, and an ordinary equation-based solver does the time stepping.
Item 3 puts it in the data representation, so one pretrained model can handle meshes. Items 4 to 6 are about getting more accuracy
from limited data and tuning effort.

This week had no software release that I could confirm. The newest PhysicsNeMo release notes still list 26.08 as the latest numbered
version (covered in [issue 1](issue-01.html)), so there is no release item.[^pnemo] Sourcing note: for every paper below I opened
the arXiv abstract page, and the summaries rely on the abstracts only. I have not read the full papers, so details beyond the
abstracts are left out.

## Concept of the week: projection-based reduced-order models

A discretised PDE is a very large system of ODEs, $\dot{u} = f(u)$, with $u \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and $N$ often in the millions. A
**reduced-order model** (ROM) bets that the solution actually lives close to a much smaller subspace. If the columns of
$V \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times r}$ span that subspace ($r \ll N$), write

$$u(t) \approx V\,a(t), \qquad \dot a = V^{\top} f(V a),$$

where $a(t)\in\mathbb{R}^r$ are the *reduced coordinates*. The second equation is the **Galerkin projection**: take the original
equation and keep only the part that points inside the subspace. You now solve $r$ equations instead of $N$, and the
equations are still the physical ones.

The classical recipe (for example, proper orthogonal decomposition) fixes $V$ from simulation snapshots. That works poorly when
features move, such as shocks or travelling fronts, because no single small subspace covers every position. The fix is a
**time-dependent subspace** $V(t)$ that follows the solution. Item 2 learns that $V(t)$ with a neural operator.

## 1. Numerical Neural Operator: derive the network from the scheme

**Paper:** *Numerical Neural Operator: Connection Between Numerical Analysis and the Operator Learning and Building Discretized Neural Operators from Finite Element Methods*. Zecheng Zhang, arXiv, 8 Oct 2026.[^nno]

**The idea in plain words.** Neural operators are trained on data that a numerical solver produced. The author asks what the
trained network actually approximates: the true infinite-dimensional operator, the training data, or the *discretised scheme* that
made the data. The working hypothesis is the last one, namely that a trained operator may mimic the numerical scheme behind its data.
If so, it makes sense to build the network from that scheme directly.

**What's technically new.** For a class of PDEs, the architecture is derived from a numerical method in two steps. First, the
basis functions of the finite element method are approximated or directly constructed by learnable networks. Second, the finite
difference scheme for time stepping is unrolled into an iterative network that generates the coefficients of those basis
functions. Because the network is a numerical scheme in disguise, standard numerical analysis gives convergence error estimates.[^nno]

**Result.** The abstract states that the network size depends *polynomially* on the $L_2$ approximation error. This improves the
nested exponential dependence derived for general neural operator approximation, and the author says it matches numerical observations.
Experiments show reduced errors and better parameter efficiency; the abstract gives no numbers.[^nno]

> **Why it matters:** Approximation theory for neural operators often gives pessimistic size requirements. A construction
> that follows a known scheme gives a much milder bound, at the price of a restricted class of PDEs. How well it carries over to
> nonlinear, turbulent problems is not addressed in the abstract.

## 2. Neural-operator-predicted reduced subspaces

**Paper:** *Neural-Operator-Predicted Time-Dependent Reduced Subspaces for Projection-Based Simulation of Nonlinear PDEs*. Boxi Song, Rennie Mirollo, Jan R. Engelbrecht, arXiv, 6 Oct 2026.[^rom]

**The idea in plain words.** Pure data-driven surrogates are fast, but the authors note they "do not necessarily satisfy the governing
equations during inference". This hybrid keeps the equations. A neural operator takes the initial condition and outputs a
time-dependent reduced subspace $V(t)$ (see the concept above). The online solver then evolves the few reduced coordinates
by solving the governing equations projected onto that moving subspace.

**What's technically new.** The network predicts an adaptive reduced *representation* rather than the full trajectory, so the
time stepping stays physics-based. The split of labour is the contribution: learning handles the geometry of where the solution lives, and
the solver handles the dynamics.[^rom]

**Result.** Tested on two nonlinear PDEs, the viscous Burgers' equation and the Fisher-KPP reaction-diffusion equation. The authors
report "substantial wall-clock speedup" relative to the full-order solver, with relative errors on the order of $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-1}$
across the tested resolutions.[^rom]

> **Why it matters:** The errors are modest, and both test problems are one-dimensional benchmarks, so this is a proof of concept
> rather than a CFD result. The design is the interesting part: a surrogate that still obeys the equations at inference time is
> easier to trust than one that does not.

## 3. Mesh-Native Convolution: foundation models on unstructured meshes

**Paper:** *Mesh-Native Tokenisation: Retrofitting Foundation Models to Unstructured Data*. Georg Maierhofer, Miles Cranmer, Payel Mukhopadhyay, arXiv, 8 Oct 2026.[^mnc]

**The idea in plain words.** Physics foundation models first turn a field into **tokens**, the latent vectors a transformer
processes. A convolutional encoder does this cheaply on regular grids. Real engineering geometry, such as curved boundaries, is
usually on **unstructured meshes**, which today usually means graph neural networks. Those cost more to train and encode information
differently, so a model pretrained on grids cannot simply be moved to meshes.

**What's technically new.** *Mesh-Native Convolution* (MNC) is a locality-aware tokenisation layer for mesh data that is analogous
to a CNN. A model can be trained once on mesh data and then deployed on arbitrary unstructured meshes, even irregular ones, with minimal
retraining. The authors also give theoretical results on consistency with classical CNN encoders.[^mnc]

**Result.** Retrofitting a grid-pretrained foundation model with MNC "in many cases reaches acceptable accuracy even in zero-shot
deployment", and outperforms existing baselines after only a few epochs of fine-tuning. The abstract gives no numbers or model names.[^mnc]

> **Why it matters:** Most of the pretrained physics models covered so far (see the [timeline](../releases.html)) assume grids. A drop-in
> tokeniser for meshes could make them usable on industrial geometries. The result is qualitative in the abstract, so the paper's tables matter here.

## 4. Cross-domain pretraining for steady-state CFD surrogates

**Paper:** *Cross-Domain Pretraining for Steady-State Neural CFD Surrogates*. Anthony Zhou, Amir Barati Farimani, Shirley Ho, Rudy Morel, arXiv, 7 Oct 2026.[^cdp]

**The idea in plain words.** A CFD surrogate usually fails on geometries and applications outside its training set, and the standard
remedy is to run more costly solver simulations for each new application. The authors instead pretrain on datasets that differ in geometry, boundary
conditions, and fidelity, and ask whether this helps on held-out datasets.

**Result.** It does. Cross-domain pretraining improves zero- and few-shot performance on held-out datasets compared with both training
from scratch and transferring from domain-specific experts. In particular, fine-tuning a pretrained cross-domain model reaches
**2-3x lower errors at the same sample size**, or **the same error with 8x fewer samples**, than training from scratch. The benefit is
architecture-agnostic and grows with model size and with pretraining dataset diversity. The authors also find that simply pooling
steady-state datasets is sufficient and effective.[^cdp]

| Compared with training from scratch | Reported result |
|---|---|
| Error at the same sample size | 2-3x lower |
| Samples needed for the same error | 8x fewer |

<small>From the abstract.[^cdp] The paper is 43 pages with 25 figures; per-dataset numbers are in its tables.</small>

> **Why it matters:** CFD data is expensive. "Just pool the datasets" is a cheap recipe, and it echoes the pretraining theme of
> issue 3 for steady flows. The abstract covers steady-state problems only, not time-dependent flows.

## 5. F3NO: split by frequency, predict at chosen times

**Paper:** *F3NO: Frequency-Decomposed Finite-Time Flow-map Neural Operators with Cross-Scale Conditioning*. Fan Wu, Cheng Jing, Kookjin Lee, arXiv, 7 Oct 2026.[^f3no]

**The idea in plain words.** Repeated one-step predictions accumulate error (the rollout drift from [issue 1](issue-01.html)), and
fine-scale structures are hard to resolve. F3NO attacks both. Each layer has two branches, global spectral processing for
low-frequency content and local refinement for high-frequency detail. The updated low-frequency features *guide* the nonlinear
refinement of the high-frequency part, which the authors call cross-scale conditioning.

**What's technically new.** The model is a *finite-time flow map*: it directly predicts the state at a specified future time and adjusts
the contribution of the two branches according to the prediction interval. For long trajectories, it predicts in parallel within short
time segments and propagates recursively between segments.[^f3no]

**Result.** On five PDE benchmarks, the authors report improved forecasting accuracy over autoregressive and direct-prediction baselines.
Ablations show the frequency split can improve accuracy with fewer parameters, while the benefit of segmentation "depend[s] on spatial
resolution and dynamical regime". The abstract gives no numbers.[^f3no]

> **Why it matters:** The honest caveat in the ablations is useful: the segmentation trick is not a free win. It is the same
> family of ideas as Transolver-σ in issue 3, which also mixes spectral and local processing.

## 6. When an LLM tunes the fine-tuning

**Paper:** *Prior or Feedback? What an LLM Uses When Adapting Neural Operators*. Julian Chan, Javier Mora Jimenez, arXiv, 8 Oct 2026 (accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on AI for Science).[^llm]

**The idea in plain words.** Here an LLM acts as the hyperparameter tuner. Given a limited trial budget, it selects fine-tuning
configurations for a pretrained neural operator moving to a new PDE. The paper's real question is *why* it works: does the LLM just use
a prior from the task description, or does it respond to the validation scores it sees?

**Result.** Across transfers within and between PDE families, the LLM achieves lower held-out test nRMSE (normalised root mean
squared error) than random search and Bayesian optimisation in nearly every matched comparison. Controlled interventions probe the
mechanism. Its first configuration, chosen before seeing any score, already ranks near the top of the corresponding random-search pool,
and the chosen base learning rate shifts with the PDE description. Once scores are available, reassigning validation scores among evaluated
configurations changes the next proposal in every case tested, while a rewrite that preserves values has no comparable aggregate effect. The authors
conclude that the LLM combines a task-dependent prior with sensitivity to feedback.[^llm]

> **Why it matters:** Fine-tuning pretrained PDE models has many knobs, and tuning them is a real cost. The causal tests are the valuable
> part, since a better final score alone cannot show what the LLM is using. The abstract does not give the size of the improvement.
