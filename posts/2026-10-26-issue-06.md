This issue covers roughly the last two weeks, because the past seven days brought no new model or tool release that I could
verify against a primary source. The common thread is the **interface** of a neural operator: what goes in, in what form, and
how the output is checked. Four of the papers change the input side (derivative features, a spline map from the mesh, explicit
conditioning, a fixed basis). The fifth asks whether we are checking the output in the right way.

## Concept of the week: what a neural operator actually sees

A neural operator learns a map from an input function to an output function, for example from a geometry and a set of flow
conditions to a pressure field. In practice the network never sees a function. It sees a **finite list of numbers**, and the choice
of what those numbers are matters a lot. Three common choices appear this week.

**1. Point values.** The default is a list of pairs $(x_i, u(x_i))$: where you sampled, and the value there. Everything the
network needs to know about derivatives or structure it must work out itself.

**2. Values moved onto a regular grid.** Fast architectures such as the Fourier neural operator (FNO) need data on a uniform grid,
because they use the fast Fourier transform. Engineering meshes are not uniform, so something has to map mesh values onto a grid
and back. A common tool is a *B-spline*: a smooth, locally supported basis function $B_i(x)$ with $\sum_i B_i(x) = 1$ (the
"partition of unity" property), so a field is reconstructed as $u(x) \approx \sum_i c_i B_i(x)$.

**3. Global parameters.** Freestream Mach number, angle of attack, or design variables are *numbers*, not fields. The usual trick
is to append them to every point's input. A more explicit option is **FiLM** (feature-wise linear modulation), where a small
network turns the parameters $c$ into a scale and shift applied to the hidden features $h$ of a layer:

$$h' = \gamma(c) \odot h + \beta(c).$$

The four methods below each change one of these choices.

## 1. ∂-NO: give the operator derivatives, and learn their order

**Paper:** *Fractional is Better: Learnable Derivative Orders in Neural Operator Learning*. Mehouachi & Jabari, arXiv, Oct 14, 2026 (accepted at ICML 2026).[^dno]

**The idea in plain words.** A PDE solution operator involves derivatives of its input. Yet most neural operators are given only raw
values $(x, u(x))$, so the network must learn to differentiate on its own. The authors ask: what if the derivatives are supplied?

**What's technically new.** Using Picard iteration on mild solutions, the paper shows that derivatives of the input appear explicitly
in the solution operator. It then proves that supplying derivative features improves the approximation bound from
$O(W^{-(s-m)/d})$ to $O(W^{-s/d})$, where $W$ is the parameter count, $s$ the input regularity, $m$ the PDE order and $d$ the
spatial dimension. The surprise is that the *best* derivative order $\beta^*$ is **strictly less than the PDE order** $m$. The authors
attribute this to a bias-variance trade-off in spectral space, which they characterise in closed form. So ∂-NO adds **learnable fractional-order
derivative features** to any neural operator backbone and lets the data choose $\beta$.

**Result.** Across benchmark problems and architectures, ∂-NO "consistently improves prediction accuracy", and the learned orders
reflect known physics modulated by noise and finite-sample limits.[^dno] The abstract gives no single headline number, so see the
paper's tables (the abstract mentions 13) for per-benchmark gains. Code is linked from the arXiv page.

> **Why it matters:** It is an *architecture-agnostic* add-on, so it can be tried on a model you already have. The finding that the
> optimal order is below the PDE order is also a useful warning: "exact" derivative features are not automatically the best ones in
> the presence of noisy data.

## 2. L-SplineFNO: a spline bridge between meshes and grids

**Paper:** *Learnable Splines for Unstructured Neural Operators*. Gopakumar, Wang, Gray, Duruisseaux, Williams, Ellis, Nobre, Field, Panayis, Blair, Davis, Pamela, Akers, Deisenroth, Anandkumar, arXiv, Oct 15, 2026.[^spline]

**The idea in plain words.** FNOs are efficient but assume a uniform rectangular grid, while engineering simulations use large,
irregular, locally refined meshes. L-SplineFNO places an FNO on a regular **latent grid** and connects it to the real mesh with a
sparse, learnable tensor-product **B-spline** transfer, in both directions (see the figure above).

**What's technically new.** A *local normalised scatter* avoids solving a geometry-dependent system to build the spline transform, while
keeping compact support, partition of unity and spline smoothness by construction. The network is conditioned on global design and
operating parameters and learns the map from geometry to the field. Because the decoder is an analytic spline, it also gives
closed-form spatial derivatives and, via Bernstein convex hulls and Taylor models, rigorous prediction bounds over continuous space
and uncertain parameters.

**Result.** On six three-dimensional engineering benchmarks the authors report strong accuracy with substantially less memory and
compute than baseline operators, scaling to **26.6 million nodes in training and 46 million in inference on a single A100 GPU**. The
bounds are demonstrated on a **29.2-million-node MAST-U tokamak model**. It also supports zero-shot super-resolution.[^spline]

> **Why it matters:** Memory, not accuracy, is often what stops neural operators from handling industrial meshes. A decoder that is
> both smooth and analytic is also rare: it opens the door to *guaranteed* bounds rather than just test-set errors.

## 3. CoPiT: make the operating conditions explicit

**Paper:** *CoPiT: A conditional Position-induced Transformer for aerodynamic field prediction on unstructured meshes*. Chen, Fan, Qiao & Wu, arXiv, Oct 23, 2026.[^copit]

**The idea in plain words.** In many surrogates, freestream conditions and design variables are simply concatenated with each point's
coordinates, so their effect on the internal features is "implicit". CoPiT instead builds on the Position-induced Transformer (PiT)
for geometry and injects a global condition vector through **FiLM** layers at several stages of the network (the formula in the
concept section). The geometry-based attention weights stay as they were.

**Result.** The model is tested on 2D AirfRANS airfoil fields (including scarce-data and extrapolation settings) and on 3D BlendedNet++
blended-wing-body surfaces. On the AirfRANS full-data test set, CoPiT reduces surface and volume losses by **approximately 54% and 24%**
relative to Transolver, and it reaches the lowest overall normalised test MSE among the compared baselines on BlendedNet++.[^copit]

| | Compared with | Reported change |
|---|---|---|
| AirfRANS surface loss | Transolver | about −54% |
| AirfRANS volume loss | Transolver | about −24% |
| BlendedNet++ normalised test MSE | Compared baselines | lowest overall |

<small>All values from the paper's abstract.[^copit]</small>

The matched ablations are the most honest part: FiLM conditioning "generally improves upon input concatenation, while the preferred
placement is task-dependent". So there is no single best recipe yet.

> **Why it matters:** Parametric design sweeps are what aerodynamic surrogates are for, and the parameters are exactly the inputs that
> concatenation handles worst. The change is small, cheap and easy to copy into other backbones.

## 4. Neural basis method: representation, then projection

**Paper:** *Solving and Learning Navier-Stokes Dynamics with the Neural Basis Method*. Wang, Wang & Wang, arXiv, Oct 23, 2026.[^nbm]

**The idea in plain words.** Physics-informed networks minimise a PDE residual, but a small residual does not guarantee a small
error in the solution, and training gets harder as problems grow. NBM follows a "representation-then-projection" principle. A set of
**fixed neural bases** defines the space the solution lives in, and **projecting the governing equations** onto it determines the
expansion coefficients.

**What's technically new.** The same bases serve as common coordinates for the whole parametric family of solutions, so operator
learning becomes a **parameter-to-coefficient map** with no explicit dependence on space. For incompressible flow, the method yields a
velocity-only formulation whose residual provides an *a posteriori velocity-error estimate*. That makes a "certified, data-free training
objective" for learning the coefficients.[^nbm]

**Result.** The abstract states that theory and experiments show accuracy, efficiency, generalisation and scalability "with
substantial improvements over baselines", but gives no numbers, so I do not quote any here. Treat it as a method to watch until the
tables are checked.

> **Why it matters:** The link between residual and error is the central weak point of physics-informed training. A formulation where
> the residual *is* an error estimate is in the same spirit as residual-based error bounds for certified surrogates, though it is a
> separate line of work.

## 5. A call to treat turbulence as four-dimensional

**Paper:** *Let's Do Turbulence Right: In Space and Time!* Plattner, Paischer & Brandstetter, arXiv, Oct 23, 2026.[^turb]

This one is a position paper backed by data. The authors use time-resolved direct numerical simulations that **exceed 28 TB per run** to
study how numerical choices shape the resolved (3+1)-dimensional physics, and then how neural networks represent the recorded
motion. Their finding is that "spatial and temporal assessments can disagree for both numerical records and learned
representations." In other words, a model or dataset that looks right when you check each snapshot in space can still be wrong in
time, and the reverse. They therefore ask the community to treat turbulence as a four-dimensional phenomenon.

I have only read the abstract, so I cannot say which metrics or models were tested. The takeaway for a practitioner is simple: report
**temporal** diagnostics (spectra in time, correlations across snapshots) alongside spatial ones.

> **Why it matters:** Most turbulence-surrogate benchmarks score spatial fields at fixed times. If spatial and temporal checks can
> disagree, a model can pass the first and still fail the second.
