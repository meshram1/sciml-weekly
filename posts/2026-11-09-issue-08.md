Four of this week's five items ask the same question from different angles: **when a surrogate gives you an answer, what
exactly are you entitled to believe about it?** One paper proves a statistical guarantee can fail, one measures how an
uncertainty estimate degrades off-distribution, one produces a guarantee that a computer proof checker accepts, and one tests
a model against real laboratory data. The fifth is about training neural operators more cheaply.

I found no new named model or tool release this week that I could verify from a primary source, so this issue has no releases section.

## Concept of the week: coverage, and why exchangeability matters

An error bar is a promise. The most useful promise is **coverage**: if a method says "90% interval," then about 90% of the time
the true value should fall inside it.

**Conformal prediction** is a standard recipe for producing intervals with a coverage guarantee and almost no assumptions
about the model. The recipe is:

1. Hold out a *calibration set* of $n$ examples the model was not trained on.
2. For each, compute a *score* $s_i$ measuring how wrong the model was (for example the absolute error).
3. Take the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile $\hat q$ of those scores, with a small finite-sample correction.
4. For a new input, report every value within $\hat q$ of the model's prediction.

If the calibration examples and the new example are **exchangeable** (roughly: drawn from the same distribution, in no
special order), the interval covers the truth with probability at least $1-\alpha$, whatever the model is. This is a standard
result in the conformal literature, not a claim from this week's papers.

The catch is the word *exchangeable*. A time-dependent PDE surrogate produces a sequence of states, and the state at time $t+1$
is strongly tied to the state at time $t$. Calibrating at one time and predicting at another is a **distribution shift**, and the
guarantee no longer applies as stated. Item 1 studies exactly this.

A second ingredient is **calibration of a probabilistic model**: does a predicted 80% interval contain the truth 80% of the time?
Item 2 shows this can hold in distribution and still fail when the test data differ from the training data.

## 1. Conformal prediction for time-dependent PDEs

**Paper:** *Conformal Prediction for Time-Dependent PDEs*. Stiller, Schneider & Hüllermeier, arXiv, Nov 5, 2026 (v2 Nov 6).[^conf]

**The idea in plain words.** If you use a neural surrogate for flood forecasting or thermal control, you want error bars you can
rely on. Conformal prediction is attractive because it makes no assumptions about the model. This paper asks whether its guarantees survive
when the thing being predicted is a PDE solution evolving in time.

**What's technically new.** It is a theory paper with three results, stated in the abstract:

- In a **function-space** setting, distributions of the solution at arbitrarily close times can be **mutually singular**,
  meaning they live on essentially non-overlapping sets. Then exact coverage guarantees are impossible.
- In **discretised** settings, the total variation distance between those distributions often **saturates exponentially fast
  with grid resolution**. The authors conclude this *mandates restrictions on the spatial discretisation* to ensure coverage.
- For certain settings, **weighted conformal prediction** gives finite-sample coverage guarantees over growing time horizons.

**Result.** The abstract reports theorems rather than benchmark numbers, so there is nothing to quote beyond the three statements above.

> **Why it matters:** Finer grids are normally a good thing. This work suggests that, for coverage guarantees, resolution can
> work against you, because finer grids make the time-shifted distributions easier to tell apart. It is a reason to be careful when a
> surrogate's "calibrated" error bars are validated at one time and used at another.

## 2. Bayesian Kolmogorov–Arnold networks for Reynolds-stress closures

**Paper:** *Bayesian Kolmogorov-Arnold networks for trustworthy Reynolds-stress closures*. Eidi, Jigjid, Buchanan & Dwight, arXiv, Nov 5, 2026.[^bkan]

**Background.** In RANS (Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes) simulation, the effect of turbulent fluctuations on the mean flow is
captured by the **Reynolds stress**, which has to be modelled. Data-driven closures learn that model from high-fidelity data. The authors
name two obstacles: the learned relations are hard to inspect, and the predictions lack reliable uncertainty estimates.

**The idea in plain words.** Use a **Kolmogorov–Arnold network** (KAN), a network whose edges carry learnable one-dimensional
spline functions instead of fixed activations, so the learned functions can be plotted and, where simple enough, approximated
symbolically. Then make it Bayesian, with a distribution over the spline coefficients.

**What's technically new.**

- A **tensor-basis** formulation enforces Galilean and rotational invariance, symmetry, and tracelessness of the stress.
  Physical realisability is handled separately, through soft regularisation.
- The predictive distribution **separates epistemic uncertainty** (from limited training data) **from aleatoric uncertainty**
  (irreducible scatter in the high-fidelity targets at given inputs).
- On a controlled regression benchmark, putting the posterior on the spline coefficients gave "the best overall balance of
  accuracy, calibration, parameter cost, and interpretability."
- The framework is extended to **3D wind-farm wakes**, where posterior samples are propagated through the RANS solver to get
  uncertainty bounds on the predicted wake.

**Result.** In distribution the closure is accurate, with calibrated and informative uncertainty that tends to flag larger errors.
Under **geometric extrapolation, the model becomes overconfident**: epistemic uncertainty rises, but not enough to account for the
growing error. On wind-farm wakes, the learned correction transfers to unseen layouts.[^bkan]

> **Why it matters:** The honest part of this abstract is the overconfidence finding. An uncertainty estimate that is well
> calibrated on test data drawn like the training data can still understate the risk on a new geometry, which is the case engineers care
> about. Read it next to item 1: both say that coverage claims depend on how the test case relates to the calibration case.

## 3. A neural-network solution with a machine-checked certificate

**Paper:** *Rigorously Certified Neural-Network Computation of Nonlinear Water Waves at Near-Machine Precision*. Shavit, Wang & Buckmaster, arXiv, Nov 5, 2026.[^cert]

**The idea in plain words.** Neural networks usually give approximate answers with no proof that they are accurate. Here, a
physics-informed neural network (PINN, trained to minimise the PDE residual, with no training data) is used purely as a
**search tool** to find candidate solutions. A separate, rigorous pipeline then checks one of them.

**What's technically new.** The pipeline has three stages:

1. **Discovery.** PINNs learn the wave profile, wave speed and, in continuation experiments, surface tension for periodic
   gravity-capillary water waves. A staged training and refinement procedure reaches **near-machine-precision residuals**
   even in strongly nonlinear regimes. One formulation recovers high-amplitude Stokes waves, Crapper waves and Wilton ripples.
2. **Certification.** One candidate is frozen as an explicit finite representation, and a uniform bound on its governing-equation
   residual is certified with **interval arithmetic**, accounting for floating-point roundoff, Fourier truncation, aliasing
   and grid-to-continuum errors.
3. **Formal verification.** The deductions from the certified numerical bounds to the residual theorem are formalised and
   machine-checked in **Lean**, a proof assistant.

**Result.** Continuation from a high-amplitude Wilton ripple in surface tension "reveals new high-amplitude candidate solutions."
The certified object is a bound on the residual of one frozen candidate.[^cert]

**A point worth noting.** The authors state that the interval certification and the Lean formalisation were developed with
substantial assistance from a large language model, while the validity of the result does not depend on it: the interval
bounds are rigorous enclosures and the Lean kernel checks the deductions.

> **Why it matters:** This is a template for using a neural network where an unreliable tool is acceptable, as a generator of
> candidates, and then handing the trust question to a method with a proof. Note that the abstract certifies a small residual for one
> candidate; I have not read the paper closely enough to say how that bound translates to distance from a true solution.

## 4. A physics foundation model, tested on laboratory turbulence

**Paper:** *Emergent Transfer of a Physics Foundation Model from Simulation to Laboratory Turbulence*. Mukhopadhyay, Nixon, Watteaux, McCabe, Bietti, Cho, Diaconu, Espejo Morales, Fouhey, Golkar, Hehir, Ho, Kovalic, Krawezik, Lanusse, Marwah, Morel, Pettee, Qu, Shen, Sotoudeh, Dalziel & Cranmer. arXiv, first submitted May 31, 2026; v2 posted Nov 4, 2026.[^walrus]

Note that this is a revised version of an earlier preprint, not a new submission.

**Background.** The **Rayleigh–Taylor instability** (RTI) occurs when a lighter fluid accelerates into a heavier one: small
perturbations at the interface grow into chaotic mixing. A long-standing puzzle is that the late-time mixing growth rate
$\alpha$ measured in most laboratory experiments (about 0.06 to 0.07) is roughly **three times** the value from idealised direct
numerical simulation (DNS, about 0.02). The abstract notes that the origin of the gap is still debated.

**The idea in plain words.** Take **Walrus**, a foundation model for continuum dynamics (see the [timeline](../releases.html)),
fine-tune it on **three or fewer DNS realisations**, and then run it on laboratory data it has never seen.

**Result (from the abstract).**

- Fine-tuned on three or fewer DNS runs, the model recovers key RTI physics over long rollouts.
- Applied **zero-shot to sliding-barrier laboratory data**, it leaves the DNS-like regime and **enters the observed growth
  band**, having seen no experimental samples.
- The authors read this as independent, data-driven evidence that **initial conditions** play a crucial role in the
  simulation–experiment gap.
- It also generalises zero-shot to **stable stratification**, a buoyancy regime absent from training, and correctly slows
  mixing-layer growth.[^walrus]

> **Why it matters:** The result is a test against reality rather than against more simulation. It also pairs with
> the previous draft issue (7), which covers a study of how Walrus forgets its pretraining when fine-tuned on simulated data.
> The interpretation about initial conditions is the authors' claim; I have summarised it from the abstract and have not
> assessed the experimental comparison myself.

## 5. WaSP: solver-free training without ignoring distribution shift

**Paper:** *WaSP: Wasserstein Data Synthesis Pipeline for Solver-Free Operator Learning*. Liu, Wang, Baptista, Stuart & Anandkumar, arXiv, Nov 5, 2026.[^wasp]

**The problem.** Training a neural operator normally needs many input–solution pairs, each from an expensive solver. The abstract
names two solver-free alternatives and their weaknesses:

| Approach | How it works | Weakness named in the abstract |
|---|---|---|
| Physics-informed learning | Minimise the PDE residual | The residual norm must be strong enough to control solution error, but stronger norms increase mini-batch variance and hinder optimisation |
| Manufactured data | Pick artificial solutions, apply the PDE operator to get matching inputs | The training distribution can be **mutually singular** with the target distribution, giving poor accuracy on it |

**The idea in plain words.** Manufacturing data is cheap because applying a PDE operator to a chosen function needs no solve.
The trouble is that the inputs you get this way don't look like the inputs you will actually be asked about. WaSP closes that gap
gradually.

**How it works.** WaSP builds a **path of intermediate distributions** converging to the target input distribution.
Training proceeds in stages along the path. At each stage, the previous surrogate generates solutions, and the **exact PDE
operator derives the corresponding inputs**, giving training pairs for the next surrogate. The authors prove a stepwise
error-propagation bound for the distribution shift introduced during synthesis.

**Result.** WaSP reduces solution error by **3.9× to 9.5×** compared with existing solver-free methods on PDEs such as
Allen–Cahn, Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and Ginzburg–Landau, and better reproduces the **statistical observables** of the two chaotic systems.[^wasp]

> **Why it matters:** For problems where generating solver data is the bottleneck, this narrows the gap between solver-free and
> solver-trained operators. The same word, *mutually singular*, appears in this paper and in item 1, which is a coincidence worth
> noticing: in both, the difficulty is that two distributions that look close can have essentially no overlap.
