The theme this week is **knowing where a learned model is wrong and doing something about it**. Two papers do that
directly: OAS finds the states where a turbulence surrogate fails and fixes them with extra simulation data, and TaiJi learns
where each weather model is most trustworthy. The Koopman papers do the same job in theory, by putting numbers on how wrong a
data-driven approximation can be.

This was a thin week for software releases. I found no new release of the tools I track, and the latest PhysicsNeMo release notes still
list 26.08 as the newest numbered version (covered in issue 1).[^pnemo] Sourcing note: for every item below I opened the arXiv
abstract page, and the summaries rely on the abstracts. I have not read the full papers, so details beyond the abstracts are left out.

## Concept of the week: Koopman operators and EDMD

A nonlinear system $x_{t+1} = F(x_t)$ is hard to analyse. The Koopman idea is to stop tracking the state $x$ itself and track
**observables**, meaning functions $g(x)$ of the state such as $x_1^2$ or $\sin x_2$. The **Koopman operator** $\mathcal{K}$
says how any observable evolves in one step:

$$(\mathcal{K}g)(x) = g\big(F(x)\big).$$

Even if $F$ is nonlinear, $\mathcal{K}$ is *linear*. The price is that it acts on a space of functions, which is infinite-dimensional.

**Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)** is the standard way to approximate it from data. You pick a finite
**dictionary** of observables $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_N$ and collect $M$ snapshot pairs $(x_m, F(x_m))$. Then you solve a
least-squares problem for a matrix $K$ such that

$$\psi(F(x_m)) \approx K\,\psi(x_m).$$

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $K$ give growth rates, frequencies, and modes of the dynamics, which is why the method
appears in fluid-flow analysis and reduced-order modelling. Two questions matter in practice. How many samples $M$ do
you need before $K$ is reliable? And can you afford a large dictionary $N$? Item 5 addresses both.

## 1. OAS: supervise the surrogate where it leaves the attractor

**Paper:** *Staying on the Attractor: Supervising Neural Surrogates of 3D Turbulence Where They Leave It*. Yilong Dai, Shaswata Mitra, Raj Patel, Yiming Sun, Shengyu Chen, Jiaqi Gong, Sudip Mittal, Shahram Rahimi, Xiaowei Jia, Runlong Yu, arXiv, 26 Sep 2026.[^oas]

**The idea in plain words.** A turbulent flow, once spun up, wanders around a bounded set of states called the **attractor**.
Training data (DNS, or direct numerical simulation) mostly lies on that set. A surrogate makes small errors, which push it
slightly *off* the attractor, into states it has barely seen. Its predictions there are poorly constrained, so errors grow and
the rollout blows up, freezes, or drifts statistically. This is the same drift mechanism as in the "why neural rollouts drift"
concept from [issue 1](issue-01.html).

OAS (off-attractor supervision) finds those off-attractor states and asks the real solver what happens next from each one.

**What's technically new.** Three generators pick a few hundred states to relabel:

1. states collected from the surrogate's **own rollouts**;
2. states from **attacks** on the surrogate, aimed at freezing, excessive amplification, and violations of incompressibility and energy balance;
3. training states **perturbed** along an amplified direction and a strongly damped random direction of the dynamics.

Each chosen state is paired with its own future computed by DNS. The attacks run only on the surrogate, and the DNS relabelling
is done offline, once per selected state.[^oas]

**Result.** On $128^3$ turbulence, OAS raises the median time to failure from 21 to 721 steps, while the compared baselines reach
medians of at most 110 steps. The advantage holds across training seeds. OAS also has the lowest pointwise error at step 15 and the
best long-horizon statistics among the compared methods.[^oas]

> **Why it matters:** Most fixes for rollout drift add noise or unroll more steps. OAS instead spends a small amount of solver time
> (a few hundred states) exactly where the model is weakest. That is a cheap, targeted use of expensive DNS, and the idea is
> not specific to turbulence. I could not find a code link on the abstract page.

## 2. TaiJi: learn which weather model to trust, and where

**Paper:** *TaiJi: State-Conditional Adaptive Combination of Machine-Learning and Physics-Based Global Weather Forecasts*. Jiale Wang, Yining Li, Guihua Wang, arXiv, 25 Sep 2026.[^taiji]

**The idea in plain words.** No single weather model wins on every variable, level, lead time, or region. Instead of training yet
another large model, TaiJi trains a **lightweight convolutional network** that looks at the recent atmospheric state and, at every
grid point and lead time, outputs **weights** for combining existing forecasts plus an additive residual correction.

**What's technically new.** The pool of forecasts mixes ML and physics-based systems: Pangu-Weather, GraphCast, FuXi, IFS-HRES,
and the IFS-ENS ensemble mean. Because the weights depend on the state, this is more flexible than a fixed average. The authors
report that the learned weights vary coherently with latitude, season, and lead time.[^taiji]

**Result.** All numbers below are the authors' own, from the abstract.[^taiji]

| | TaiJi |
|---|---|
| Training cost | about 8 hours on two RTX 4090 GPUs, at least an order of magnitude below any ML constituent |
| Protocol | WeatherBench 2, 2020 test year |
| Skill | beats the strongest baseline for each variable and lead time across eight core variables, up to 10 days, in RMSE and ACC |
| Win rate | near 100% |
| Extremes | advantage preserved for tropical-cyclone tracks and heavy precipitation |

> **Why it matters:** If it holds up, a lab without a supercomputer can improve on the best available forecasts by post-processing
> them. As the abstract says, this builds on existing models rather than replacing them. A caveat: the paper is a preprint, and one
> test year is a limited sample.

## 3. West-WRF AI 2-km: a stretched grid for atmospheric rivers

**Paper:** *West-WRF AI 2-km: High-Resolution Prediction of Integrated Vapor Transport and Precipitation*. Nazak Rouzegari, Vesta Afzali Gorooh, Agniv Sengupta, Phu Nguyen, Kuo-Lin Hsu, Amir AghaKouchak, Soroosh Sorooshian, F. Martin Ralph, Luca Delle Monache, arXiv, 22 Sep 2026.[^ww]

**The idea in plain words.** Rain in the western US is driven by **atmospheric rivers** (long plumes of moist air) hitting
mountains. Global AI models at coarse resolution smear out the narrow coastal bands where the rain falls. This model uses a
**stretched grid**: about 2 km over the western US and part of the Northeast Pacific, and about 31 km elsewhere.

**What's technically new.** It starts from a global model pretrained on 40 years of ERA5 reanalysis, then is fine-tuned on the CW3E
2-km regional reanalysis. It makes autoregressive 6-hourly forecasts of precipitation and **integrated vapor transport** (IVT, the
total moisture flux in a column of air).[^ww]

**Result.** Evaluated over winters 2020-2023 against gridded precipitation, rain gauges, and AR Reconnaissance dropsondes, and
benchmarked against coarser AI forecasts and regional and global NWP. The authors report sharper narrow coastal precipitation
bands, retained fine-scale spectral variability, and precipitation-intensity distributions that match observations. Large-scale
skill is comparable to the coarser configurations. At the most extreme IVT threshold, dropsonde verification shows lower errors
and better categorical skill. The abstract gives no headline numbers, so I do not quote any.[^ww]

> **Why it matters:** Rare, local extremes are what flood managers care about, and they are what coarse models blur. A single
> model with a fine patch and a coarse rest-of-world is a practical way to buy resolution where it matters.

## 4. AFDONet: designing a neural operator from theory

**Paper:** *Theory Guided and Interpretable Neural Operator Design for Partial Differential Equation Learning*. Zeyuan Song, Zheyu Jiang, arXiv, 27 Sep 2026 (37 pages).[^afd]

**The idea in plain words.** Most neural operator architectures are found by trial and error. AFDONet is built from an existing
mathematical framework, **adaptive Fourier decomposition (AFD)**, which represents a function with a small number of adaptively chosen
basis elements. This is placed inside a variational autoencoder (VAE) structure to solve a general class of nonlinear PDEs on
smooth manifolds.

**Result.** The abstract claims high accuracy and competitive efficiency on several benchmarks, and better performance for PDEs on
arbitrary Riemannian manifolds and for data with sharp gradients. It does not give numbers, so I cannot report any here. The claim
that this is the first neural PDE solver fully guided by an established mathematical framework is the authors' own.[^afd]

> **Why it matters:** Interpretability and a clear link between architecture and theory are rare in this area. Whether the accuracy
> claims survive independent comparison against FNO-style baselines is the open question, so treat this as one to watch.

## 5. Two papers that put error bars on EDMD

Both use the setup from the concept section above.

**Finite-data bounds.** *Finite-Data Error Bounds for Approximating the Koopman Operator: Sampling Measures, Super-Polynomial Convergence and Regularization*. Daniel Fassler, Rachel Morris, Jason Bramburger, Simone Brugiapaglia, arXiv, 25 Sep 2026 (10 pages, 3 figures).[^kfin]
Much earlier theory says EDMD converges as the data grows without limit. This paper proves bounds for a *finite* number of samples $M$. The rate is proportional to $1/\sqrt{M}$ (the Monte Carlo rate). It applies to discrete and continuous dynamics, including stochastic
systems. In certain more structured settings, super-polynomial rates are achievable, in theory and in computation. The authors also give
recovery guarantees for an EDMD variant with few samples, inspired by compressed sensing.

**Fourier dictionaries.** *Extended dynamic mode decomposition with Fourier dictionaries: Error bounds and fast implementation*. Felix Bartel, Sandra Ritter, Manuel Schaller, Karl Worthmann, arXiv, 17 Sep 2026.[^kfour]
On a $d$-dimensional torus with data on an equispaced grid and a Fourier dictionary, the EDMD regression has a unique closed-form
solution, which the authors show equals trigonometric interpolation of the Koopman image. That gives error bounds of optimal order
with explicit constants. It also means the EDMD matrix never has to be assembled: it can be applied with non-equispaced fast Fourier transforms at quasi-linear cost
in the dictionary size. They demonstrate it on the Kuramoto model of coupled oscillators with dictionaries of up to $10^7$ modes.

| | Finite-data bounds | Fourier dictionaries |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | How many samples are enough? | How large a dictionary can we afford? |
| Setting | General: discrete/continuous, stochastic | Torus, equispaced grid, Fourier dictionary |
| Main result | Error $\propto 1/\sqrt{M}$; faster in structured cases | Optimal-order bounds; matrix-free evaluation |

> **Why it matters:** Koopman methods are often used with little guidance on data requirements. Bounds like these are what
> turn a heuristic into a method you can size and budget. Both results are theoretical settings, though, and neither is a
> statement about turbulent flows.
